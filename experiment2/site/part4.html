Part 4 · the central test

If you'd followed the “best” wallets, would it have worked?

This is the whole point. Rank wallets on everything known up to a date, then measure whether the top-ranked keep winning in a disjoint later window — against random, median, and bottom-decile baselines. Then check if that edge is a repeatable skill, what its equity curve looks like, and how fast it decays.

A · THE DESIGN

Cumulative rank, disjoint forward evaluation

For each month t from Jul 2025 to Mar 2026: rank wallets on all positions resolved through t (cumulative → enough sample, since holders resolve slowly), then evaluate them only on positions resolved in (t, t+3 months].

Why evaluation must be disjoint: the cumulative ranking windows overlap each other, so consecutive rankings look sticky by construction. Only a disjoint forward window — positions that contributed nothing to the ranking — is a genuine out-of-sample test. We never report ranking-to-ranking stickiness as persistence.

We restrict to directional holders (trade_holding_rate ≥ 0.5, a count-based behavioral check — n_hold_positions ÷ n_positions — plus a $10 minimum average position size to exclude micro-betting bots) — the followable population — and rank by four different metrics, because which metric persists is itself the finding.

Two methodology notes, both from a 2026-08-14 review of this part: (1) the holder filter used to be the profit-weighted trading_share — Part 2's key variable for a different question (where profit came from), which turned out to misclassify 91-98% of behaviorally-active traders as "holders" when reused as a behavioral filter. (2) The test itself used to be a Spearman rank correlation between historical rank and forward ROI — a claim about whether ordering is preserved across the whole population, not about whether an individual wallet's own edge persists. Both are fixed below; see EXPERIMENT2_GUIDE.md for the full writeup.

4A · PERSISTENCE

Yes — modest, but real in every window

The test: good_past = wallet's historical metric was positive; good_fwd = its forward ROI was positive in the disjoint window. We report P(good_fwd｜good_past) vs. P(good_fwd｜bad_past) vs. the unconditional baseline, averaged across 9 monthly windows, with a chi-square significance test on the gap between the two conditional rates:

Rank byP(good⏐good_past)P(good⏐bad_past)baselinegapwindows sig.

Every metric ranks wallets so a historically-good wallet is more likely than baseline to keep a positive edge forward — and for the strongest metric the gap is significant in all 9 windows. Two clean results:

  • Forecasting skill persists best. holding_excess_return (did they beat the price they paid) has the largest, most reliably significant gap.
  • Conviction-sizing barely beats baseline on returns. sizing_gap → forward ROI is significant in only 11% of windows — a specific hypothesis, tested and mostly rejected. The sizing behavior itself is a stable trait, though (sizing_gap → fwd_sizing_gap: 67% vs. 53%, significant in 67% of windows) — it just doesn't reliably pay off.

The signal moves roughly symmetrically in both directions: a good-past wallet is about 10 points more likely than baseline to stay good; a bad-past wallet is about 5-7 points less likely. Avoiding bad wallets and picking good ones are comparably useful — neither dominates.

The good_past/bad_past gap is positive in every single window (never a lucky one-off); p-values from 2.6e-05 to 0.042.

4D · DECAY

The persistence rate fades gradually — no clean half-life

The same good_past/good_fwd test as 4A, evaluated at 1, 3, and 6-month horizons instead of one fixed 3-month window:

horizonP(good⏐good_past)P(good⏐bad_past)gaptop-decile fwd ROIedge vs medianwindows sig.

The good_past/bad_past gap shrinks gradually — from +20 points at 1 month to +16 points (84% of the 1-month gap) at 3 months to +13 points (66%) at 6 months — but never approaches zero within this range, so there is no clean 2–3-month half-life. A good wallet stays relatively good for at least 6 months, with steadily diminishing force — re-rank every 1–3 months anyway, since the erosion is real even without a sharp cliff.

·

Synthesis

Some wallets are genuinely worth following — but the edge is small, lumpy, decays gradually over at least half a year without fully disappearing, and cuts both ways (avoiding bad wallets and picking good ones are comparably useful). A real out-of-sample signal exists, strongest via forecasting skill (not conviction sizing), concentrated in rare high-conviction bets. The ideal steady compounder is ~1% of holders.

◆ · A DATA PREREQUISITE

Where the resolution dates came from

The whole design bins positions by resolution date (rank on resolved-by-t, evaluate on resolved-in-(t, t+3]). But no resolution timestamp exists in our subgraph data. The best available proxy is the CLOB market end_date — resolution follows within days, which is plenty for monthly binning.

We pulled it from CLOB's bulk markets endpoint (paginated), stopping early once cohort coverage hit 98%. of clean resolved positions got a date.

This is a proxy, and it's the main methodological caveat of Part 4. If end_date systematically differs from true resolution, a position could be binned into the wrong month near a boundary. At monthly granularity the effect is small, but it is real.
